Topic 5 Murphy, PML — §5.1.3 ROC curves

Four rates, one threshold, one curve

A classifier that outputs a score still has to draw a line somewhere: above it, say "positive." Every choice of line produces a different 2×2 table of hits and mistakes. The ROC curve is what you get when you stop picking one line and plot all of them at once.

5.1.3.1

The confusion matrix and its four rates

Rows are what's true, columns are what the classifier said. The four rates all come from dividing a cell by its row total — that is, conditioning on the true label. So they pair up: TPR + FNR = 1 across the top row, FPR + TNR = 1 across the bottom. Two numbers, TPR and FPR, carry everything.

Sensitivity, recall
TPR = TP ⁄ (TP + FN)

Of the real positives, how many did we catch? The y-axis of the ROC curve.

Miss rate
FNR = FN ⁄ (TP + FN)

Of the real positives, how many slipped through. Always 1 − TPR.

False-alarm rate
FPR = FP ⁄ (FP + TN)

Of the real negatives, how many did we wrongly flag? The x-axis of the ROC curve.

Specificity
TNR = TN ⁄ (FP + TN)

Of the real negatives, how many we correctly left alone. Always 1 − FPR.

5.1.3

ROC lab: type a table, watch the curve

Enter any counts. The rates update, and your table becomes one point on the ROC plot. One point alone isn't a curve — so the lab assumes the classic textbook model, where the scores of negatives and positives are two unit-variance Gaussians a distance d′ apart. Your point pins down d′, which pins down the whole curve. Then slide the threshold to move along it.

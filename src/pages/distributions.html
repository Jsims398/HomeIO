The four distributions you'll keep meeting

Bernoulli for a coin, categorical for a die, Gaussian for anything that got averaged, and the empirical distribution for "I have no model, only data." Plus the softmax — the little function that turns any pile of numbers into a categorical distribution, which is the entire output layer of every classifier we're going to build.

2.4

Bernoulli & binomial

One parameter, one coin flip. Y ∈ {0, 1} with p(Y = 1) = θ, written compactly as p(y | θ) = θy(1 − θ)1−y — the exponents are just a switch that selects the right factor. Flip N times and count the successes and you get the binomial, which is the same θ with a ( N⁄s ) out front to count the orderings.

Two bars, and they always sum to one. That constraint is why a Bernoulli has one parameter and not two.

2.5

Categorical, and the softmax

The categorical is the Bernoulli with more than two faces: C probabilities, non-negative, summing to one. The awkward part is that a model doesn't naturally produce numbers like that — a neural net hands you arbitrary real-valued logits. The softmax fixes that in two moves: exponentiate (now everything is positive) and divide by the total (now it sums to one).

pc  =  ezc / T  ⁄  Σc′ ezc′ / T

T is the temperature. It doesn't change the ranking — only how strongly the winner is favoured.

Shift invariance
softmax(z + c) = softmax(z)

The constant cancels top and bottom. That's why implementations subtract max(z) first — same answer, no overflow.

T → 0
argmax, hard

All the mass moves to the largest logit. This is the "greedy decoding" end of the dial.

T → ∞
uniform, 1/C

The logits stop mattering. Sampling here is maximally random — the "creative but incoherent" end.

2.6.4

Why the Gaussian is everywhere

Murphy gives four honest reasons, and none of them is "nature is Gaussian." It has just two interpretable parameters; the central limit theorem makes sums of anything drift toward it; it makes the fewest assumptions of any distribution with a given mean and variance (maximum entropy); and it's mathematically the path of least resistance. It's the default because it's cheap and defensible, not because it's true.

μ and σ, that's it

Shaded band is μ ± σ — about 68% of the mass, wherever you put it. The curve moves and widens; the shape never changes.

2.7.6

The empirical distribution

The most honest distribution there is: put a spike of mass 1/N on each data point you actually observed, and zero everywhere else. No parameters, no assumptions, no smoothing. Its cdf is a staircase that jumps 1/N at every sample — and as N grows it presses in on the true cdf, which is the whole reason bootstrapping works.

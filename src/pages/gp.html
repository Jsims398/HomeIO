Topic 12 Murphy, PML — §17.2.1 – 17.2.2 Gaussian process regression

A bell curve over whole functions

A Gaussian process (GP) starts with a cloud of plausible curves and lets the data rule out the ones that don't fit. The setup: read any curve at two inputs and you get two numbers (f(x₁), f(x₂)). Across all plausible curves those pairs form an oval-shaped Gaussian, tilted more when x₁ and x₂ are close (the tilt is K(x₁, x₂), the kernel). Learning from data is then one move: slice that oval. 17.2.1 does it with exact observations, 17.2.2 with noisy ones.

① Learning = slicing ② Exact data ③ Noisy data
17.2.1

Noise-free observations: learning = slicing the oval

Big idea
Observing f(x₁) means keeping only the curves that pass through it

In the oval picture, "we saw f(x₁) = a" means: throw away every dot not on the vertical line f(x₁) = a. The dots left on that line tell you what f(x₂) can still be. That leftover slice is a smaller Gaussian, and it is the posterior. Nothing is "fit"; we just condition.

Formula, decoded · one point
μ = K₁₂ ⁄ K₁₁ · a   σ² = K₂₂ − K₁₂² ⁄ K₁₁

K₁₂ ⁄ K₁₁ is how strongly f(x₂) follows f(x₁): close to 1 if x₂ is near x₁, close to 0 if far. K₁₂² ⁄ K₁₁ is how much uncertainty the observation removes. This is just the Gaussian conditioning rule (§3.2.3) with 2 variables.

Formula, decoded · the book's version
μ* = K*,X KX,X−1 fX   Σ* = K*,* − K*,X KX,X−1 KX,*

Same thing with many points. X = training inputs, * = test inputs. KX,X−1 plays the role of "÷ K₁₁". The mean is a blend of the observed values; the covariance is the prior's minus what the data explained.

Drag left–right on the oval (or use the slider) to change the observed value a. The coloured bell on the slice is the posterior of f(x₂). On the left, the bar at x₂ is the same bell, seen from the side. The whole band is that slice, repeated at every x₂.

Why doesn't the uncertainty depend on a?

Look at σ² = K₂₂ − K₁₂² ⁄ K₁₁: there is no a in it. For a Gaussian, every slice of the oval has the same width; only its centre moves. So a GP's error bars depend on where you have data, never on what values you saw. (That is a real limitation, and one reason people tune the kernel to the data.)

17.2.1

Lab: exact data pins every curve down

What you're looking at
Three posterior curves, a mean, and a band

The thin curves are random draws from the posterior: functions the GP still believes after seeing the data. The thick curve is their average μ*, and the band is μ* ± 2 sd. Every draw must pass through every point, because noise-free means the point is the function.

The book calls it
An interpolator

The mean goes exactly through the data and the variance is zero there. Between points, the uncertainty grows; far from all data it returns to the prior (mean 0, ± 2σf). Useful when observations really are exact, like outputs of a slow but deterministic computer simulation.

Why does the mean always drift back to 0 far from data?

Far from every point, K*,X ≈ 0: the test input is not similar to anything we saw. Then μ* = K*,X(…) ≈ 0 = the prior mean, and Σ* ≈ K*,* = the prior variance. With no similar data, the GP falls back on what it believed before.

17.2.2

Noisy observations: stop forcing the curve through the dots

Big idea
We see y = f(x) + ε, not f(x) itself

Real measurements wobble: ε ∼ 𝒩(0, σy²). Two readings at nearly the same x can disagree, and a noise-free GP would twist itself into knots to hit both. With noise, the GP is allowed to miss each dot by about σy, so it goes between them.

Formula, decoded · the only change
KX,X → Kσ = KX,X + σy² I

Noise is independent for each reading, so it only adds to the diagonal (each y's own variance), never to the cross terms. Then μ* = K*,X Kσ−1 y and Σ* = K*,* − K*,X Kσ−1 KX,*. In the oval picture, the oval gets stretched along the y axis, so slices get wider. (Go back to the slice demo and raise σy.)

One test point · a weighted sum
μ(x*) = Σn wn yn, w = Kσ−1 k*

Like Nadaraya–Watson in Topic 11, the prediction is a weighted sum of the observed y's. But these weights come from Kσ−1, which knows the training points overlap each other. Two near-duplicates share a vote, and weights can go negative.

f vs. a new y
Two different error bars

Σ* is the uncertainty about the true curve f(x*). If you want to predict the next measurement y*, add the noise back on: 𝕍[y*] = Σ* + σy². That band can never shrink below ± 2σy, however much data you have.

Why can a weight be negative?

Suppose the query sits just past dot A, and dot B lies just past A on the far side. The GP reads A and B together as a slope: if B is higher than A, the curve is rising toward B, so at the query (on the other side of A) it should be lower than A. Giving B a negative weight does exactly that extrapolation. Nadaraya–Watson only averages, so it can't follow a trend this way.

Is the "noise-free" GP just the noisy one with σy = 0?

Yes. Every formula in 17.2.2 turns into 17.2.1 when σy = 0. In code people still add a tiny σy (a "jitter", here 10−6 or less) because KX,X can be almost impossible to invert when two inputs are very close. That near-singular matrix is exactly what makes the dashed curve explode.

17.2.1 – 17.2.2

Recap: two cases, one formula

SectionThe pictureThe formula
17.2.1 Noise-freeObserving data = slicing the cloud. Curves are pinned through every point; the band pinches to zero there.μ* = K*,X KX,X−1 fX
17.2.2 NoisyAdd σy² to the diagonal. The curve goes between the dots, the band never pinches, and the mean is a weighted sum of the y's.μ* = K*,X (KX,X + σy² I)−1 y

The catch: Kσ−1 is an N × N inverse, which costs about N³ operations. Fine for a few thousand points, painful for a million. And the results depend heavily on ℓ, σf and σy; choosing them from the data comes later in the chapter.

How wrong, and how jumpy

Both readings are secretly about the same knob. A mixture model asks how many components K; a polynomial fit asks what degree. Turn either one up and the model can express more — but it also starts chasing the particular noise in the particular data you happened to collect. That tension has a name and, better, an exact decomposition.

3.5

Mixture models — complexity you choose

Take a hidden label z ∈ {1…K} drawn from a categorical with weights π, then draw x from whichever component z picked. Marginalize the label away and you get a convex combination of simple distributions that, together, can be arbitrarily flexible.

p(x | θ)  =  Σk=1K πk   pk(x | θk)

πk ≥ 0, Σk πk = 1 — the weights are themselves a categorical, straight out of §2.5.

GMM
pk = N(x | μk, Σk)

K Gaussian blobs, each with its own centre and its own ellipse — the same Σ from Topic 3. The workhorse for clustering continuous data.

Mixture of Bernoullis
binary features

Each component is a product of Bernoullis with its own θk. Murphy's example is binarized MNIST: each component learns a prototype digit.

Responsibility
rnk = πk pk(xn) ⁄ Σj πj pj(xn)

Bayes' rule again, posterior over the hidden label. Soft clustering: a point can be 70% component 2 and 30% component 5.

K
This is where the two readings meet

K = 1 can only ever draw one blob — badly wrong, but it says the same thing no matter which sample you hand it. K = N puts a component on every data point — perfectly faithful to this sample, useless on the next one. Everything below is that trade-off, made precise.

The bias–variance lab

Read the two error terms straight off the picture. Bias² is the gap between the dashed average fit and the sage truth — at degree 0 or 1 the average is nowhere near the curve, and no amount of data will fix that. Variance is how widely the faint curves fan out — at degree 8 or 9 each one wiggles off after its own handful of points, and the average can still look fine while every individual fit is bad. The third term is the noise floor: even the true function scores σ² on fresh data.

The curve

Why test error is U-shaped

Sweep the degree and plot all three terms. Bias falls monotonically, variance climbs, and the sum — the thing you actually pay — dips somewhere in the middle. That dip is the model you want, and it moves right as you collect more data.

Try it
More data moves the dip

Push points-per-set to 40. Variance collapses — each fit has enough evidence to stop guessing — so the optimum shifts toward higher degree. Complexity you can afford is a function of sample size.

Try it
Set σ to zero

The noise floor vanishes and so does most of the variance: with clean data, overfitting has nothing to overfit to. The trade-off is driven by noise, not by flexibility alone.

The catch
You only get one sample

Bias and variance are expectations over training sets you'll never see. That's why the practical version is a validation set or cross-validation — estimating the black curve without being able to draw the faint ones.

Probability, and why you should always switch doors

This week was the probability crash course: what a probability actually is, how random variables package up uncertainty, what happens when you have two of them at once, and Bayes' rule — the one tool that turns "here's what I saw" into "here's what I now believe." Then I put the last one to work on the game show.

2.1

Basics of probability

Two readings of the same number, and Murphy says pick whichever helps. The frequentist one: a probability is the long-run fraction of times something happens if you repeat the experiment forever. The Bayesian one: it's a degree of belief about something that may only happen once. Coin flips work fine under either; "will it rain tomorrow" only works under the second, and that's the reading the rest of the chapter runs on.

Union
P(A ∨ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∧ B)

Add them, then subtract the overlap you just double-counted. If A and B can't both happen, the overlap is 0 and it's plain addition.

Joint & conditional
P(A ∧ B) = P(A | B) P(B)

The product rule. Read it as: first B happens, then A happens given that. Rearranged, P(A | B) = P(A ∧ B) / P(B).

The move that matters
Conditioning shrinks the world

P(A | B) isn't a new kind of quantity — it's the same probability measured inside a smaller sample space, the one where B is already true. Every surprise in this chapter comes from forgetting to shrink.

2.2 – 2.2.2.2

Random variables

A random variable is just a labelled outcome: a function from the sample space to numbers. The useful split is discrete vs continuous, because it changes what object you're allowed to write down.

Discrete
pmf: p(x) = P(X = x)

Finite or countable values. Each one gets real mass, the masses are between 0 and 1, and they sum to 1. Asking "what's the chance X is exactly 3?" is a fair question.

Continuous
cdf P(X ≤ x), pdf p(x) = d/dx P(X ≤ x)

Any single value has probability zero, so the honest object is the cdf; the pdf is its derivative and can exceed 1. Probability lives in intervals — areas under the curve, not heights.

The thing I keep having to re-learn: a density is not a probability. p(x) = 4 is legal. P(X = x) = 4 is not.

2.2.3 – 2.2.4

Joint distributions & independence

With two variables, the joint p(x, y) is the whole story — every other quantity is something you squeeze out of it. Sum a row or column away and you get a marginal. Fix a row and renormalize it and you get a conditional.

Independence
X ⊥ Y  ⇔  p(x, y) = p(x) p(y)

Unconditional. Knowing Y tells you nothing about X, and the joint table is exactly the outer product of its own margins.

X ⊥ Y | Z  ⇔  p(x, y | z) = p(x | z) p(y | z)

Conditional. Once you know Z, the dependence dissolves — this is the assumption every graphical model in the rest of the course is built out of.

Neither one implies the other, in either direction. Two variables can be dependent overall and independent given Z, or independent overall and dependent given Z.

2.3

Bayes' rule

One line of algebra from the product rule, and the engine of the whole book: it flips a conditional you know into the one you want.

p(H | E)  =  p(H) · p(E | H)  ⁄  p(E)
Posterior
belief after seeing E
Prior
belief before
Likelihood
how well H predicts E
Evidence
Σh p(h) p(E | h)

The denominator is the part people skip and then get wrong. It's not a mystery constant — it's the sum of the numerator over every hypothesis, which is exactly what makes the posterior add up to 1. And in the door problem below, it's the entire ballgame.

Visualization

Three doors, one car

You pick a door. The host — who knows where the car is — opens a different door and shows you a goat. Now: stay or switch? Your gut says it's 50/50 between the two closed doors. Your gut is conditioning on the wrong thing.

!
The host's rules are the whole puzzle

He always opens a door, never yours, and never the car. Those three constraints are what make the reveal informative. Change any one of them — a host who opens at random, say — and switching stops helping.

The same answer, out of §2.3

Let C be the car's door and H the door the host opens. You picked door 1; he opened door 3. We want p(C = 2 | H = 3).

Prior p(C = 1) = p(C = 2) = p(C = 3) = 1/3 — nothing seen yet, so all three are on equal footing.
Likelihood p(H = 3 | C = 1) = 1/2  — both goats are open to him, he picks one.
p(H = 3 | C = 2) = 1  — door 1 is yours, door 2 hides the car; he has no choice.
p(H = 3 | C = 3) = 0  — he never opens the car.
Evidence p(H = 3) = 1/3·1/2 + 1/3·1 + 1/3·0 = 1/2
Posterior p(C = 2 | H = 3) = (1/3 · 1) ⁄ (1/2) = 2/3
p(C = 1 | H = 3) = (1/3 · 1/2) ⁄ (1/2) = 1/3

The prior was uniform, so the whole result is carried by the likelihood: the host's reveal was twice as likely in the world where the car sits behind door 2. Your own door never got new evidence — it's still 1/3 — and all of the freed-up belief piles onto the one remaining door.

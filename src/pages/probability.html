Probability, and why you should always switch doors

This week was the probability crash course: what a probability actually is, how random variables package up uncertainty, what happens when you have two of them at once, and Bayes' rule — the one tool that turns "here's what I saw" into "here's what I now believe." Then I put the last one to work on the game show.

2.1

Basics of probability

Two readings of the same number, and Murphy says pick whichever helps. The frequentist one: a probability is the long-run fraction of times something happens if you repeat the experiment forever. The Bayesian one: it's a degree of belief about something that may only happen once. Coin flips work fine under either; "will it rain tomorrow" only works under the second, and that's the reading the rest of the chapter runs on.

Union
P(A ∨ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∧ B)

Add them, then subtract the overlap you just double-counted. If A and B can't both happen, the overlap is 0 and it's plain addition.

Joint & conditional
P(A ∧ B) = P(A | B) P(B)

The product rule. Read it as: first B happens, then A happens given that. Rearranged, P(A | B) = P(A ∧ B) / P(B).

The move that matters
Conditioning shrinks the world

P(A | B) isn't a new kind of quantity — it's the same probability measured inside a smaller sample space, the one where B is already true. Every surprise in this chapter comes from forgetting to shrink.

Exam notes · 4-point version

Union and conditioning on a 4-sided die

Data: one fair 4-sided die, Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4}, each outcome 1/4. Events: A = "roll a 4" = {4}, B = "roll ≥ 3" = {3, 4}.

  1. Count. P(A) = 1/4, P(B) = 2/4 = 1/2, overlap A ∧ B = {4} so P(A ∧ B) = 1/4.
  2. Union. P(A ∨ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∧ B) = 1/4 + 1/2 − 1/4 = 1/2. Check by listing: A ∨ B = {3, 4}, 2 of 4 outcomes ✓.
  3. Conditional. P(A | B) = P(A ∧ B) / P(B) = (1/4) / (1/2) = 1/2.
  4. Read it as shrinking the world. Once you know B, only {3, 4} are left, and one of those two is a 4: 1/2. Knowing B doubled P(A) from 1/4 to 1/2.
  5. Product rule check. P(A ∧ B) = P(A | B) P(B) = 1/2 · 1/2 = 1/4 ✓.

Say it in one sentence: Union adds the two probabilities and subtracts the double-counted overlap; conditioning on B throws away every outcome outside B and renormalizes what is left.

2.2 – 2.2.2.2

Random variables

A random variable is just a labelled outcome: a function from the sample space to numbers. The useful split is discrete vs continuous, because it changes what object you're allowed to write down.

Discrete
pmf: p(x) = P(X = x)

Finite or countable values. Each one gets real mass, the masses are between 0 and 1, and they sum to 1. Asking "what's the chance X is exactly 3?" is a fair question.

Continuous
cdf P(X ≤ x), pdf p(x) = d/dx P(X ≤ x)

Any single value has probability zero, so the honest object is the cdf; the pdf is its derivative and can exceed 1. Probability lives in intervals — areas under the curve, not heights.

The thing I keep having to re-learn: a density is not a probability. p(x) = 4 is legal. P(X = x) = 4 is not.

Exam notes · 4-point version

A pmf on 4 values, and a density bigger than 1

Discrete: a pmf on 4 values

  1. Data. X ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3} with p(0) = 0.1, p(1) = 0.2, p(2) = 0.3, p(3) = 0.4.
  2. Valid pmf? Each is between 0 and 1, and 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.4 = 1 ✓.
  3. cdf = running sum. P(X ≤ 0) = 0.1, P(X ≤ 1) = 0.3, P(X ≤ 2) = 0.6, P(X ≤ 3) = 1.
  4. A single value has real mass. P(X = 2) = 0.3 is a fair question for a discrete variable.

Continuous: Uniform(0, ¼)

  1. Density. It must integrate to 1 over a width of ¼, so p(x) = 1 / (1/4) = 4 on [0, ¼]. A density of 4 is legal.
  2. cdf. P(X ≤ x) = 4x for x in [0, ¼]; its derivative gives back p(x) = 4.
  3. Probability = area. P(0.1 ≤ X ≤ 0.2) = 4 · 0.1 = 0.4, while any single point has P(X = 0.1) = 0.

Say it in one sentence: A pmf gives each value real probability and those sum to 1, while a pdf is the slope of the cdf, so only areas under it are probabilities and its height can exceed 1.

2.2.3 – 2.2.4

Joint distributions & independence

With two variables, the joint p(x, y) is the whole story — every other quantity is something you squeeze out of it. Sum a row or column away and you get a marginal. Fix a row and renormalize it and you get a conditional.

Independence
X ⊥ Y  ⇔  p(x, y) = p(x) p(y)

Unconditional. Knowing Y tells you nothing about X, and the joint table is exactly the outer product of its own margins.

X ⊥ Y | Z  ⇔  p(x, y | z) = p(x | z) p(y | z)

Conditional. Once you know Z, the dependence dissolves — this is the assumption every graphical model in the rest of the course is built out of.

Neither one implies the other, in either direction. Two variables can be dependent overall and independent given Z, or independent overall and dependent given Z.

Exam notes · 4-point version

Joint, marginal, conditional, independence: 2 × 2 = 4 cells

Data: X = rain (0/1), Y = umbrella (0/1). Joint table p(0,0) = 0.4, p(0,1) = 0.1, p(1,0) = 0.1, p(1,1) = 0.4.

  1. Marginal: sum out the other variable. p(X=1) = 0.1 + 0.4 = 0.5, p(Y=1) = 0.1 + 0.4 = 0.5.
  2. Conditional: fix a row, renormalize. p(Y=1 | X=1) = 0.4 / 0.5 = 0.8. Rain makes the umbrella likely.
  3. Independence test. Is p(1,1) = p(X=1) p(Y=1)? 0.5 · 0.5 = 0.25 ≠ 0.4, so X and Y are dependent.
  4. An independent table, for contrast. p = [0.3 0.3 ; 0.2 0.2] has margins X: 0.6, 0.4 and Y: 0.5, 0.5, and every cell equals the product, e.g. 0.6 · 0.5 = 0.3 ✓.

Conditional independence is not independence

  1. Setup. A hidden Z ∈ {0, 1}, each with prob ½. Given Z = 0, X and Y are independent coins with P(1) = 0.2; given Z = 1, independent coins with P(1) = 0.8. So X ⊥ Y | Z by construction.
  2. Joint without Z. p(X=1, Y=1) = ½ · 0.2² + ½ · 0.8² = 0.02 + 0.32 = 0.34.
  3. Marginals. p(X=1) = ½ · 0.2 + ½ · 0.8 = 0.5, same for Y, so the product is 0.25 ≠ 0.34: X and Y are dependent overall, even though they are independent given Z.

Say it in one sentence: The joint table is everything: sum out a variable to get a marginal, fix a row and renormalize to get a conditional, and the variables are independent exactly when every cell equals the product of its margins.

2.3

Bayes' rule

One line of algebra from the product rule, and the engine of the whole book: it flips a conditional you know into the one you want.

p(H | E)  =  p(H) · p(E | H)  ⁄  p(E)
Posterior
belief after seeing E
Prior
belief before
Likelihood
how well H predicts E
Evidence
Σh p(h) p(E | h)

The denominator is the part people skip and then get wrong. It's not a mystery constant — it's the sum of the numerator over every hypothesis, which is exactly what makes the posterior add up to 1. And in the door problem below, it's the entire ballgame.

Exam notes · 4-point version

Bayes' rule with 4 hypotheses

Data: a bag with 4 coins whose heads-probabilities are θ = 0, 1/3, 2/3, 1. Pick one at random, flip it once: heads. Which coin is it?

  1. Prior. Picked at random: p(θ) = 1/4 for each coin.
  2. Likelihood. p(heads | θ) = θ, so 0, 1/3, 2/3, 1.
  3. Evidence = sum of prior × likelihood. p(heads) = ¼(0 + 1/3 + 2/3 + 1) = ¼ · 2 = 1/2.
  4. Posterior = prior × likelihood / evidence. (¼ · θ) / ½ = θ/2: 0, 1/6, 1/3, 1/2.
  5. Check. 0 + 1/6 + 1/3 + 1/2 = 1 ✓. The always-tails coin is ruled out, and the always-heads coin is now the best guess.

Say it in one sentence: The posterior is prior times likelihood divided by the evidence, and the evidence is just that same product summed over every hypothesis so the posterior adds to 1.

Visualization

Three doors, one car

You pick a door. The host — who knows where the car is — opens a different door and shows you a goat. Now: stay or switch? Your gut says it's 50/50 between the two closed doors. Your gut is conditioning on the wrong thing.

!
The host's rules are the whole puzzle

He always opens a door, never yours, and never the car. Those three constraints are what make the reveal informative. Change any one of them — a host who opens at random, say — and switching stops helping.

The same answer, out of §2.3

Let C be the car's door and H the door the host opens. You picked door 1; he opened door 3. We want p(C = 2 | H = 3).

Prior p(C = 1) = p(C = 2) = p(C = 3) = 1/3 — nothing seen yet, so all three are on equal footing.
Likelihood p(H = 3 | C = 1) = 1/2  — both goats are open to him, he picks one.
p(H = 3 | C = 2) = 1  — door 1 is yours, door 2 hides the car; he has no choice.
p(H = 3 | C = 3) = 0  — he never opens the car.
Evidence p(H = 3) = 1/3·1/2 + 1/3·1 + 1/3·0 = 1/2
Posterior p(C = 2 | H = 3) = (1/3 · 1) ⁄ (1/2) = 2/3
p(C = 1 | H = 3) = (1/3 · 1/2) ⁄ (1/2) = 1/3

The prior was uniform, so the whole result is carried by the likelihood: the host's reveal was twice as likely in the world where the car sits behind door 2. Your own door never got new evidence — it's still 1/3 — and all of the freed-up belief piles onto the one remaining door.

Exam notes · 4-point version

Monty Hall with 4 doors

Data: 4 doors, one car. You pick door 1. The host knows where the car is, never opens yours or the car, and picks at random among the goat doors he may open. He opens door 4.

  1. Prior. p(C = 1) = p(C = 2) = p(C = 3) = p(C = 4) = 1/4.
  2. Likelihood of "host opens 4". Car behind 1: he can open 2, 3 or 4 → 1/3. Car behind 2: he can open 3 or 4 → 1/2. Car behind 3: he can open 2 or 4 → 1/2. Car behind 4: never → 0.
  3. Evidence. p(H = 4) = ¼(1/3 + 1/2 + 1/2 + 0) = ¼ · 4/3 = 1/3.
  4. Posterior. p(C = 1 | H = 4) = (¼ · ⅓) / ⅓ = 1/4; p(C = 2 | H = 4) = (¼ · ½) / ⅓ = 3/8; the same 3/8 for door 3; 0 for door 4.
  5. Decision. Staying wins 1/4; switching to either other closed door wins 3/8. Switching still helps, for the same reason as with 3 doors: your own door never gets new evidence.

Say it in one sentence: The host is more likely to open door 4 when the car is behind another closed door than when it is behind yours, so Bayes moves belief onto those doors and leaves yours at its prior.
